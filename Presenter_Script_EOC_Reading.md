## Menchville High School | EOC Reading Spring 2026

## SLIDE 1: Significant Gaps & Tier 1 Changes

### Opening (30 seconds)
"We've analyzed our Spring 2026 EOC Reading results across all student populations—All Students, Students with Disabilities, and English Language Learners. While our overall performance is strong at 63% for all students, three specific reading skills emerged as priority gaps where every student population fell below our 60% proficiency goal. Today we're presenting our instructional response."

### Significant Gaps Section (1 minute)

"Our data shows three skills where all populations are underperforming:

**First: Standard 10.RI.1.C — Evaluate details in a text, determining which could confirm or disprove an argument or position.**
All students are at 53%, SWD at 40%, and ELL at 32%. This is our weakest high-volume skill across every group. Students struggle to distinguish between details that support versus contradict claims—a foundational skill for informational reading.

**Second: Standard 11.RL.1.C — Analyze how characters are revealed through particular lines of dialogue or events.**
This is our most-tested standard on the exam. All students at 61%, SWD at 53%, ELL at 30%. Students know characters exist in texts, but they're not consistently analyzing what specific dialogue or events tell us about who those characters are.

**Third: Standard 11.RVN.1.B — Use context and sentence structure clues to clarify the meaning of words.**
All students at 52%, SWD at 51%, ELL at 40%. Across all populations, students are not systematically using context and sentence patterns to unlock word meaning—leaving them stuck when encountering unfamiliar vocabulary."

### Changes to Tier 1 Instruction (1.5 minutes)

"To address these gaps, we're adjusting Tier 1 instruction in three strategic ways:

**For 10.RI.1.C — Claim Check Routine**
Students need daily practice distinguishing supporting details from contradicting details. Here's what this looks like: We're implementing a weekly paired-passage task. Students receive two short texts on the same topic and decide which specific details from each text confirm the main claim versus disprove it. They justify their thinking using PEE—Point, Evidence, Explanation—or CER—Claim, Evidence, Reasoning. Before students work independently, we model our thinking: 'I read this detail... does it back up the claim or work against it? Here's why.' This makes the mental process visible.

**For 11.RL.1.C — Character Annotation Routine**
Students often summarize characters rather than analyze them. This routine flips that. When reading a passage with character interactions, students mark one line of dialogue or one event that reveals something about the character. Then they write: 'This reveals that [character] is [quality/motivation/change] because...' For SWD and ELL, we provide a graphic organizer with sentence starters: 'This dialogue shows...' or 'This event reveals...' This scaffolds the analytical thinking.

**For 11.RVN.1.B — Context-Clue Routine**
Five minutes each day. Not random vocabulary—words from the texts students are actually reading. We teach students to look at sentence structure: Do commas set off a definition? Does the next sentence explain? We model with think-alouds: 'I don't know this word, but look at the comma here... it's telling me what this word means.' For ELL students, we pre-teach key words using the Frayer Model, making connections to their home language when possible and building multiple representations of meaning."

### Monitoring & Evidence (45 seconds)

"Here's how we'll know this is working:

Every three weeks, one quick 5-item CFU—Checks for Understanding—per skill. These are given to all students, SWD, and ELL cohorts to track progress. In MP1, we're adding a benchmark assessment on all three SOLs. 

In our walkthroughs, we're looking for: Is the claim-check routine happening weekly? Are students annotating characters? Is the context-clue work showing up daily? Our PLC will review student work samples monthly—looking at the quality of their thinking, not just correctness.

This data will drive our decisions. If one skill moves faster than others, we adjust. If ELL students need additional support, we intensify. We're monitoring in real time."

## SLIDE 2: Evidence Behind the Response

### Title & Context (20 seconds)

"Here's the detailed evidence backing our instructional response. This table shows each skill, the specific performance by population, and what the research-backed strategy looks like in practice."

### Skill 1: 10.RI.1.C (45 seconds)

"**Evaluate details in a text, determining which could confirm or disprove an argument or position.**

Performance: All students at 53%—905 student responses—SWD at 40% with 118 responses, and ELL at just 32% with 25 responses. This is the weakest high-volume skill in every group, which means many students are missing this question type across the exam.

Why this matters: In informational reading, evidence distinguishes strong arguments from weak ones. Students who can't separate supporting details from contradicting details will struggle with persuasive texts, media analysis, and argument evaluation across content areas.

**Tier 1 Change: Weekly Paired-Passage Claim Check**
Students examine two texts on related topics. The task: Identify which details from each text confirm the central claim and which work against it. They explain their reasoning using PEE (Point-Evidence-Explanation) or CER (Claim-Evidence-Reasoning) structure. We begin with a teacher think-aloud so students see the decision-making process.

**Evidence Collection: **
Every three weeks, a 5-item CFU on this skill. In MP1, a benchmark assessment on 10.RI.1.C for all cohorts. This gives us weekly to monthly snapshots of growth."

### Skill 2: 11.RL.1.C (45 seconds)

"**Analyze how characters are revealed through particular lines of dialogue or events.**

Performance: All students at 61%—1,338 responses—SWD at 53% with 196 responses, ELL at 30% with 20 responses. This is the most-tested standard on the entire exam, meaning the most student responses were missed on this skill type.

Why this matters: Character analysis is foundational to literature study. But 'analyze' is different from 'identify' or 'describe.' Students must connect what a character says or does to what that reveals about who they are. Without this skill, students read plot but miss the depth of the story.

**Tier 1 Change: Character Annotation Routine**
Students mark a line of dialogue or a specific event from the text. Then they complete: 'This reveals that [character name] is [quality/motivation/emotion/change] because [evidence].' For SWD and ELL, we provide a graphic organizer and sentence starters to support the analytical move. The routine anchors students in evidence while they practice the analytical language.

**Evidence Collection: **
Annotated passages become PLC discussion pieces—monthly reviews of student samples. Three-week CFU checks. MP1 benchmark on 11.RL.1.C."

### Skill 3: 11.RVN.1.B (45 seconds)

"**Use context and sentence structure clues to clarify the meaning of words.**

Performance: All students at 52%—402 responses—SWD at 51% with 63 responses, ELL at 40% with just 5 responses. Under 60% in every single group.

Why this matters: Vocabulary knowledge predicts reading comprehension. But students who can't independently use context don't build vocabulary across reading. They get stuck, stop reading, or guess. Teaching them to read the sentence structure—'What's in the comma? What does the next sentence tell me?'—gives them a tool for every text they encounter.

**Tier 1 Change: Daily 5-Minute Context-Clue Routine**
Five minutes. Every day. Using words from texts students are actually reading in class right now. We model: 'I don't know this word. Look at the comma here—it's showing me the definition. Look at the sentence after—it explains.' For ELL, we pre-teach key vocabulary using the Frayer Model—connecting to home language, using images, giving synonyms. Multiple exposures, multiple representations.

**Evidence Collection: **
Vocabulary CFU every three weeks. MP1 benchmark on 11.RVN.1.B. Classroom observations confirm the routine is happening consistently."

## PRESENTER TIPS
- Use student-friendly language; avoid jargon when possible (define terms like "CER," "Frayer Model")
- Show examples: Have a sample paired passage, annotated character passage, and context-clue example on hand
- Emphasize routines are *daily* or *weekly*—not one-time lessons
- When discussing ELL data, acknowledge the small sample sizes (20 and 5 responses) while emphasizing the pattern of need
- Be ready to discuss specific texts/examples your department is already using
- Frame this as "continuous improvement"—we're strong, we're getting stronger
